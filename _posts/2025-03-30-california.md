A week at the end of March: Yosemite and Sequoia still under snow, the Pacific along Highway 1, then the Mojave, Death Valley and a stretch of the old Route 66. What stays with me is how many worlds California fits into one state. Granite valleys and alpine forest, a cold ocean coast, dunes, salt flats and lava fields, often only a few hours' drive apart.

## Yosemite

The alarm went off long before sunrise. The only way to have the valley almost to yourself is to be at the gate by eight, before the queue of cars builds up behind you. Yosemite Falls was already loud with snowmelt.

## Sequoia

Another alarm in the dark, for the same reason: to reach the Giant Forest by eight, while it was still quiet and still under snow.

{% include gallery.html images="/assets/img/journal/california-2025/08.jpg, /assets/img/journal/california-2025/09.jpg" ratios="0.666, 1.502" size="wide" %}

Its most famous resident, the General Sherman, is the largest tree on Earth by volume: about 1,487 m³ of wood, 83.8 m tall, 31 m round at the base, and somewhere between 2,300 and 2,700 years old. It is not the tallest. That record belongs to Hyperion, a coast redwood of about 116 m hidden in Redwood National Park, much further north on the coast.

{% include figure.html src="/assets/img/journal/california-2025/10.jpg" size="wide" %}

Giant sequoias are built for fire. Their bark can be 46 cm thick, fibrous and slow to burn, and their cones can stay shut for some twenty years until the heat of a fire dries them out and releases the seeds onto freshly cleared ground. Many of the oldest trees carry black scars from fires that came and went centuries ago. In 2021, when the KNP Complex Fire reached the grove, firefighters wrapped the base of the General Sherman in protective foil; it came through unharmed.

## Mojave

Inland again, into the Mojave National Preserve. I spent the night at a ranch inside the preserve, with wind gusts strong enough to rattle the whole building until morning.

{% include gallery.html images="/assets/img/journal/california-2025/21.jpg, /assets/img/journal/california-2025/22.jpg" ratios="0.666, 1.502" caption="Light falling into a lava tube, and me inside it." size="wide" %}

Around the lava fields grow the Joshua trees. Despite the name they are not trees but giant yuccas, in the agave family, and they cannot reproduce without yucca moths: the moth pollinates the flower while laying its eggs inside it, and its larvae pay themselves by eating some of the seeds. They grow a few centimetres a year at most and have no growth rings, so their age can only be guessed from their height. The name is said to come from Mormon settlers in the mid-1800s, who saw in the raised branches the prophet Joshua beckoning them west, although there is little record of it.

{% include gallery.html images="/assets/img/journal/california-2025/23.jpg, /assets/img/journal/california-2025/39.jpg" ratios="0.666, 0.666" caption="Joshua trees, with cinder cones on the horizon." size="wide" %}

The Kelso Dunes rise up to about 200 m above the desert floor. They are known as “booming” dunes: when the sand is dry and slides down a slip face, it gives off a low hum you can feel as much as hear. I stayed until sunset.

## Death Valley

{% include figure.html src="/assets/img/journal/california-2025/29.jpg" caption="The rental car on the way down into Death Valley." size="wide" %}

Badwater Basin lies 86 m below sea level, the lowest point in North America. Its salt flats cover some 500 km², and up close the ground is a crust of salt crystals cracked into polygons.

## Route 66

The last morning, on the old Route 66 near Amboy. A short way east of town, two marble Chinese guardian lions sit in the desert a few hundred metres off the road, a quarter of a mile apart. Nobody knows who put them there; they were first noticed around 2013. The male rests a paw on a ball, the female on a cub, and passers-by leave coins and painted stones at their feet.

{% include gallery.html images="/assets/img/journal/california-2025/35.jpg, /assets/img/journal/california-2025/36.jpg" ratios="0.666, 0.666" caption="The guardian lions of Route 66." size="wide" %}

Amboy itself is mostly one place: Roy’s Motel and Café, opened in 1938, with the boomerang-shaped neon sign that went up in 1959 and is now an icon of the old road. When Interstate 40 bypassed the town in 1972, the business, in its owner’s words, “went down to zero”.

{% include figure.html src="/assets/img/journal/california-2025/37.jpg" caption="Roy’s Motel and Café, Amboy." size="wide" %}

Behind it rises Amboy Crater, a cinder cone about 79,000 years old, standing some 75 m above its own lava field. In the Apollo years the US Geological Survey used volcanic landscapes in California like this one, the Amboy area among them, to train astronauts and test lunar rovers.

{% include figure.html src="/assets/img/journal/california-2025/38.jpg" caption="Amboy Crater and its lava field." size="wide" %}
