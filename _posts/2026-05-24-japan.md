Nine days in the middle of May, mostly on foot and by train: Tokyo, the Shinkansen west past Mount Fuji, Kyoto and Osaka, then back east to Kamakura and a last afternoon in Tokyo.

## Tokyo

The first evenings went on getting lost in the city. Omoide Yokocho, by the west exit of Shinjuku station, is a few narrow alleys of tiny bars and yakitori counters, each seating a handful of people, that began as a black market after the war.

{% include gallery.html images="/assets/img/journal/japan-2026/01.jpg, /assets/img/journal/japan-2026/02.jpg" ratios="0.666, 0.666" caption="Omoide Yokocho in Shinjuku at night; Ueno Naka-dōri, the shopping street beside Ameyoko, the next morning." size="wide" %}

Sunday morning was in Ueno, between the shopping streets and the park, and on the way to Asakusa a youth baseball game was going on in the dust and the heat.

{% include gallery.html images="/assets/img/journal/japan-2026/03.jpg, /assets/img/journal/japan-2026/04.jpg" ratios="0.666, 0.666" caption="A temple in Ueno, and a Sunday ball game." size="wide" %}

## Sanja Matsuri

We had arrived in time for the Sanja Matsuri, held every year on the third weekend of May at Asakusa Shrine, beside Sensō-ji. It honours the three men said to have founded the temple, and it is one of the largest festivals in Tokyo, with between one and a half and two million people over three days. On the Sunday the three great mikoshi, the portable shrines of Asakusa Shrine, leave at dawn and are carried through all 44 districts of the neighbourhood until the evening, each one swaying on the shoulders of dozens of bearers in matching happi coats.

In the late afternoon we crossed the river to the Tokyo Skytree. At 634 m it is the tallest tower in the world, open since 2012, with two observation decks at 350 and 450 m. From up there Tokyo has no edge: it simply carries on to the horizon. We stayed until the sun set over the city, at about half past six.

## West

The next morning we took the Shinkansen west to Kyoto. A little before eight, Mount Fuji appeared on the right side of the train, 3,776 m and still capped with snow, framed for a moment between the poles of the overhead line.

{% include figure.html src="/assets/img/journal/japan-2026/38.jpg" caption="Fuji between two poles, from the Shinkansen." size="wide" %}

## Kyoto

The first morning in Kyoto was in Arashiyama, on the western edge of the city: tall bamboo, a quiet temple with a pagoda, and the Katsura river. Its bridge, the Togetsukyō, is 155 m long. The name means “moon-crossing bridge”, after Emperor Kameyama, who watched the full moon from a boat and thought it seemed to be crossing it. The current bridge dates from 1934.

{% include figure.html src="/assets/img/journal/japan-2026/20.jpg" caption="The Katsura river and the Togetsukyō bridge, Arashiyama." size="full" %}

{% include gallery.html images="/assets/img/journal/japan-2026/21.jpg, /assets/img/journal/japan-2026/22.jpg" ratios="0.666, 0.666" caption="A pine on the riverbank; Kinkaku-ji across its pond." size="wide" %}

In the afternoon, Kinkaku-ji, the Golden Pavilion. It began as the retirement villa of the shogun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu at the end of the fourteenth century and became a Zen temple after his death. The top two floors are covered in gold leaf. The building you see today is a copy: in 1950 a young monk set fire to the original, and it was rebuilt in 1955.

{% include figure.html src="/assets/img/journal/japan-2026/23.jpg" caption="Kinkaku-ji." size="wide" %}

The next morning started early at Fushimi Inari, the shrine of Inari, the kami of rice and, by extension, of business. Most of its thousands of vermilion torii were donated by companies, and their names are painted on the uprights. Together they form tunnels that climb Mount Inari, 233 m high, with stone foxes, the god’s messengers, along the way.

{% include gallery.html images="/assets/img/journal/japan-2026/24.jpg, /assets/img/journal/japan-2026/25.jpg, /assets/img/journal/japan-2026/26.jpg" ratios="0.666, 0.666, 0.666" caption="Fushimi Inari: thousands of torii up the mountain." size="wide" %}

{% include figure.html src="/assets/img/journal/japan-2026/27.jpg" caption="Looking back over Kyoto from the climb." %}

Later that morning we walked up to Kiyomizu-dera, founded in 778, through a hillside of graves packed close together. Its three-storey pagoda, about 30 m tall and rebuilt in 1632, is one of the largest in Japan.

{% include gallery.html images="/assets/img/journal/japan-2026/28.jpg, /assets/img/journal/japan-2026/29.jpg, /assets/img/journal/japan-2026/30.jpg" ratios="1.502, 0.666, 0.666" caption="Graves on the hillside below Kiyomizu-dera, and its pagoda." size="wide" %}

## Osaka

A day in Osaka. Toyotomi Hideyoshi began its castle in 1583. It was destroyed in 1615 and rebuilt by the Tokugawa, and the main tower burned again after a lightning strike in 1665. The tower standing today is a reinforced-concrete reconstruction from 1931, with a museum inside.

{% include figure.html src="/assets/img/journal/japan-2026/31.jpg" caption="Osaka Castle." size="wide" %}

## Kamakura

Back east for a day by the sea, in Kamakura. At Hase-dera the slope is covered with rows of small stone jizō, each placed by parents mourning a child they lost.

{% include gallery.html images="/assets/img/journal/japan-2026/32.jpg, /assets/img/journal/japan-2026/33.jpg" ratios="0.666, 0.666" caption="Koi and rows of jizō at Hase-dera." size="wide" %}

A short walk away sits the Great Buddha: a hollow bronze Amida, 13.35 m tall with its base, cast around 1252. It once stood inside a hall, but storms damaged the building more than once, and in 1498 a tsunami washed the last hall away. It has sat in the open ever since.
