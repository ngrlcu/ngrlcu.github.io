For most people the Camino de Santiago is a life-changing experience. However many kilometres they walk, they seem to come home with a pack full of new things learnt and stories to tell, enough to make friends and family want to set off too. Mine officially began in Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port, with a fever of 39.5 °C. It was a beautiful experience, but not so different from other journeys I have made. If anything, my Camino was a tasting menu that lasted almost a month, with walks in between to digest one course before the next.

I divide the Camino Francés into four parts.

## Saint-Jean to Burgos

The beginning that never happened, but one day surely will. The day before setting off, a nasty bout of gastroenteritis sent me back home and the start was postponed. Time was already short, so I had to start again from Burgos, this time successfully, at the end of October.

## The Meseta: Burgos to León

About 180 km across the high plateau of Castile: long straight tracks, a wide horizon and little shade. On the second morning the path runs straight under the arches of San Antón, the ruins of a fourteenth-century convent of the Antonine friars, who cared for pilgrims suffering from "St Anthony's fire", a poisoning caused by a fungus in rye bread.

León closes the Meseta. Its Gothic cathedral, begun in the thirteenth century, has more than 1,800 m² of stained glass in over 250 windows, so that inside the walls seem made of light.

{% include gallery.html images="/assets/img/journal/camino-2026/28.jpg, /assets/img/journal/camino-2026/29.jpg" ratios="1.509, 0.75" caption="León cathedral, outside and in." size="wide" %}

## León to Sarria

Another 200 km or so. The landscape becomes more interesting, and the path more demanding.

{% include figure.html src="/assets/img/journal/camino-2026/31.jpg" caption="Out of León, under a big sky." size="wide" %}

{% include gallery.html images="/assets/img/journal/camino-2026/32.jpg, /assets/img/journal/camino-2026/33.jpg" ratios="0.75, 0.75" caption="An albergue sign nailed to a tree; autumn colours on the way up." size="wide" %}

At about 1,500 m, the highest point of the Camino Francés, stands the Cruz de Ferro: an iron cross on a tall pole, at the top of a mound of stones. Pilgrims bring a stone from home, or pick one up along the way, and leave it here.

{% include figure.html src="/assets/img/journal/camino-2026/34.jpg" caption="The Cruz de Ferro." %}

Below the mountains lies the Bierzo, a valley of vineyards that in November had turned red.

{% include figure.html src="/assets/img/journal/camino-2026/39.jpg" caption="Vineyards in the Bierzo." size="wide" %}

As every forecast predicted, Galicia welcomed us with rain. But the rain gave us a magical arrival at O Cebreiro, a stone village at about 1,300 m on the border of Galicia: low clouds in the evening and only a few lights in the village.

{% include gallery.html images="/assets/img/journal/camino-2026/40.jpg, /assets/img/journal/camino-2026/41.jpg" ratios="0.75, 0.75" caption="Under the motorway viaducts in the Valcarce valley; the stone that marks the entry into Galicia." size="wide" %}

{% include figure.html src="/assets/img/journal/camino-2026/42.jpg" caption="Galicia, the next morning." size="wide" %}

## Sarria to Santiago

The last 116 km are the most famous, most walked and most commercial part of the route, because 100 km on foot is the minimum to receive the Compostela. The main difficulty was finding a way to dry wet clothes, and shoes. But there is little climbing, so we managed a 40 km stage, from Triacastela through Sarria to Portomarín. We set off in the dark and arrived in the evening, in very low cloud and rain, with a Harry Potter atmosphere.

{% include gallery.html images="/assets/img/journal/camino-2026/45.jpg, /assets/img/journal/camino-2026/46.jpg" ratios="1.333, 1.333" caption="Leaving before dawn; fog in the valleys." size="wide" %}

{% include gallery.html images="/assets/img/journal/camino-2026/47.jpg, /assets/img/journal/camino-2026/50.jpg" ratios="0.75, 0.75" caption="One of the milestones counting down to Santiago; one morning we set off without breakfast, and after two hours and 10 km this appeared." size="wide" %}

{% include figure.html src="/assets/img/journal/camino-2026/48.jpg" caption="Arriving in Portomarín after dark, across the bridge over the Miño." size="wide" %}

The town itself was moved. In the 1960s the Miño was dammed to create the Belesar reservoir and the old village was flooded, so its Romanesque church of San Xoán was taken down and rebuilt stone by stone higher up the hill. When the water is low, the remains of the old village reappear on the riverbed.

{% include figure.html src="/assets/img/journal/camino-2026/49.jpg" caption="The reservoir below Portomarín, with the water low." %}

## Santiago

When we arrived, the sky miraculously opened above our heads for a few minutes. That was just long enough for the customary photos, and to enjoy the moment we had waited for so long.
